Part I

Why regression?

From estimation to prediction

So far: estimation

Population → Sample → Estimate

Estimate a mean, variance or proportion.

Quantify uncertainty using standard errors and confidence intervals.

Now: statistical learning

Observed data → Learn relationship → Predict

Use information about an observation to predict an outcome. Ask whether the learned relationship will work on new observations.

Key connection: a fitted regression model is itself an estimate obtained from a sample.

Motivation

Can we predict delivery time from delivery distance? Can we predict house price from size, location and age? Can we predict electricity demand from temperature and time of day?

These problems share three ingredients:

  1. an outcome we care about;
  2. information that may help predict it;
  3. previous observations from which we can learn.

Recurring example for the lecture: predicting delivery time from delivery distance.

What is statistical learning?

Working definition

Statistical learning refers to methods that use observed data to learn relationships between variables.

Two common objectives are:

Prediction: Given new values of those variables, what outcome should we expect? Explanation: How is an outcome associated with one or more variables? A regression model may be used for either purpose, but the two questions are not identical.

Prediction versus explanation

Example: delivery time and distance

Prediction

A new delivery is 10 km away. How long should we expect it to take?

Explanation

How does delivery time tend to change as distance increases?

Which variables appear to be associated with longer delivery times?

Important: good prediction does not automatically imply a causal explanation.

What 𝑅2 does not tell us

𝑅2 = 0.91 does not mean 91% of deliveries are predicted exactly — ours are still about 2.2 minutes out on average.

It does not establish that distance causes delivery time; it only says the two move together in this sample.

It is measured on the deliveries used to fit the regression line, so it says nothing on its own about new deliveries (Part V).

It is a comparison with one particular baseline. A high 𝑅2 against a weak baseline is not an achievement.

Rule of thumb

Quote 𝑅2 together with an error in the units of the outcome — “𝑅2 = 0.91, average miss 2.2 minutes” says far more than either number alone.

Generalisation

Generalisation is the ability of a fitted model to perform well on new observations that were not used to fit it.

For statistical learning, this is usually more important than achieving the smallest possible error on the training sample.

Question: Why might a model fit the training data extremely well but still predict badly on new data?

Regression models are estimates too

Our 37 deliveries are a sample. Another 37 would give a different line.

Take six samples of 12 of our deliveries and refit: the slopes range from 1.42 to 1.65, and the prediction at 10 km from 26.1 to 27.7 minutes.

So 𝑏0 and 𝑏1 are estimates, with sampling variability — exactly like a sample mean.

In this lecture we discussed…

Regression learns relationships between an outcome and one or more predictors. A fitted regression model can be used to make predictions for new observations. Residuals quantify the difference between observed and predicted values. Least squares provides a rule for fitting a simple linear regression line. Model quality should be assessed through prediction error and meaningful baselines. Training/test separation helps us assess generalisation. Underfitting and overfitting are central risks in statistical learning. Multiple regression extends the same ideas to several predictors. Regression describes statistical relationships; it does not automatically establish causality.
